
1. WHY GDG ALGIERS IS DIFFERENT FROM TRADITIONAL ADVERTISING

First, I would listen to the company's concerns and ask what happened during its previous sponsorship. I would want to understand whether the problem was low visibility, the wrong audience, or a lack of measurable results. This would help me suggest something more suitable instead of repeating the same offer.

Traditional advertising, such as Facebook ads and posters, can help a company reach a large audience. Sponsoring a GDG Algiers event offers a different opportunity: the company can connect with people who are already interested in technology.

GDG Algiers brings together developers, technology enthusiasts, and motivated students. Its online community includes more than 20,000 Facebook followers, 20,000 Instagram followers, 11,000 LinkedIn followers, and 4,000 Discord members. These figures show the community's reach, although they do not guarantee a specific number of attendees or potential customers.

There are several ways this could benefit the company:

- Relevant audience: The company can introduce its products, services, or opportunities to people interested in technology.
- Direct interaction: Depending on the event, the company could meet participants, answer questions, demonstrate a product, or take part in a discussion. This is different from someone simply seeing an advertisement.
- Recruitment and employer branding: If the company is looking for interns or future employees, the event could help it meet students and developers.
- Community relationships: The company could build connections with the local tech community and explore future activities, such as workshops or technical talks.
- Measurable results: Before the event, we could agree on realistic ways to evaluate the partnership, such as social media reach, engagement, link clicks, booth interactions, or interest in job opportunities.

I would explain that sponsorship is not automatically better than traditional advertising. The two serve different purposes, and the right choice depends on the company's goals. If the company wants to test whether an event partnership can reach the right audience, we can design a small collaboration with clear objectives.

2. IF THE COMPANY STILL SAYS NO: OFFER A SMALL ENTRY POINT

If the representative is not ready to commit to a large sponsorship, I would suggest starting with a smaller contribution. The aim would be to make the decision easier while giving both sides a chance to see whether the partnership is useful.

Possible options include:

- In-kind support: The company could provide refreshments, printing, merchandise, or prizes instead of giving a large amount of money.
- Small financial contribution: The company could cover one specific need, such as workshop materials or prizes.
- Speaker or mentor support: The company could provide a technical speaker or mentor if that fits the event program.
- Limited visibility or recruitment activity: If appropriate, the company could share internship opportunities or participate in a small information stand.

I would not offer every benefit automatically. I would first check what the event actually needs and what GDG Algiers can realistically provide.

For example, I would say:

“I understand why you are cautious, especially if your previous sponsorship did not bring the results you expected. Instead of asking you to commit to a large budget, we could start with a smaller contribution for one specific event need. Before we agree, we can define what your company hopes to achieve and what we can provide in return. After the event, we can share the results we are able to measure. This would let you evaluate the experience before considering a larger partnership.”

To keep the trial clear, I would agree with the company on:
- The contribution and what it will cover.
- The visibility or participation GDG Algiers can provide.
- The dates and deadlines.
- The results that will be measured.
- Any limits or conditions.

I would put the agreement in writing and avoid promising guaranteed sales, recruitment, or a particular return on investment. The purpose is to test the partnership fairly, not to promise results we cannot control.

3. WHEN TO STOP INSISTING

I would stop insisting when the representative gives a clear final refusal, especially after I have listened to their concerns and offered a reasonable alternative. Repeatedly pushing after that point could damage the relationship and make GDG Algiers appear desperate.

I would also step back if the company's expectations do not match what we can deliver, if it rejects all reasonable options, or if its requested conditions could harm the community's experience or reputation.

Before ending the discussion, I would thank the representative for their time and leave the door open for future contact. For example:

“Thank you for explaining your concerns. I understand that this may not be the right opportunity for your company at the moment, and I respect your decision. If your priorities change in the future, we would be happy to discuss a collaboration that fits your objectives. Thank you again for your time.”

Afterwards, I would record the outcome and focus on other companies that are a better fit. Stopping does not mean the negotiation failed; it means respecting the company's decision and using our time wisely.
